いろばこ

Japanese only. ば is は with two short marks at its shoulder — the dakuten — and those two marks are now cherry-blossom petals, notch and all: one the blue of the goi flower, one the pink of the kanji flower.

on paper

on goi’s black

on the kanji site’s pink

vertical, and shrunk down

the petal, close up — note the notch

Notes

What makes it sakura. A cherry-blossom petal is not a plain oval — it has a small notch cut into the tip, and that notch is the whole difference between reading as a blossom and reading as a leaf. It is cut deep enough here to still show once the petal shrinks to accent size.

The petals do the work. Those two marks are exactly what turns iroha into irobako, so the blossom is the accent rather than an ornament placed beside it. Set vertically they stay at the upper right of the は, which is where dakuten belong in vertical Japanese anyway.

Why not kanji. 色箱 also reads irobako and looks more formal, but kanji has no は for the marks to sit on, so the petal idea disappears.

Worth checking on your screen: I placed and sized the petals by measuring against a real ば, but there is no browser here, so I checked it in a different Japanese font rather than Shippori Mincho. If they sit too high, too low, or crowd the こ, say which and it is a one-line nudge.
